A Nation of Immigrants

Almost everyone in the United States can trace their family back to somewhere else. Here's the long history behind today's immigration system — and where things stand right now.

Section 1

Colonial Era & Early Immigration

Before there was a United States, before any ship from Europe ever crossed the Atlantic, people already lived here. Indigenous peoples had been living across North America for thousands of years — hunting, farming, building towns, and trading across huge networks that stretched from coast to coast. Archaeologists have found tools and artifacts in Virginia alone dating back more than 15,000 years.NPS By the time the first English colonists showed up in the late 1500s and early 1600s, Native nations had complex societies, governments, and cultures already in place. So the story of immigration to America doesn't start with the first immigrant. It starts with the people who were already home.

That's an important thing to keep in mind as you read the rest of this page. When European colonists began arriving, they weren't moving into an empty land — they were moving into land that belonged to someone else. The Library of Congress puts it plainly: European colonization of North America "was an invasion of territory controlled and settled for centuries by Native Americans."LOC

Colonists arrive, mostly from England

Starting in the early 1600s, small numbers of Europeans began crossing the Atlantic to build permanent settlements. The English founded Jamestown, Virginia in 1607 and Plymouth, Massachusetts in 1620. Around the same time, the French built Quebec, and the Dutch set up colonies in what's now New York.LOC Over the next several decades, companies like the Massachusetts Bay Company sent thousands of colonists — including whole families — across the ocean.LOC

Most of these early arrivals came from England, but other European groups came too, in smaller numbers — including the Dutch, French, Germans, and Scots-Irish. This pattern of mostly-English, some-other-European immigration continued through the colonial period and into the early years of the new United States. Nearly all of these people came by choice, looking for land, religious freedom, or a fresh start.

Key Word — Voluntary Immigration

Moving to a new country by choice — deciding for yourself to leave one place and settle in another. This is different from being forced to move against your will.

A very different story: the transatlantic slave trade

Not everyone who came to the American colonies had a choice. Starting in 1619 — a year before the Pilgrims even landed at Plymouth — enslaved Africans were being brought to England's Jamestown colony in chains.LOC This was the start of what's known as the transatlantic slave trade: European traders kidnapped and purchased African people, packed them onto ships, and transported them across the Atlantic Ocean to be enslaved. This was forced migration, not immigration — people did not choose to come, and once they arrived, they were not free.

The scale of this forced migration was enormous. Historians estimate that over the centuries-long span of the transatlantic slave trade, more than 10 million people were enslaved and transported from Africa to the Americas. Of those, several hundred thousand were brought specifically to Britain's American colonies and, later, the United States. An unknown number — but likely more than a million people — died during the brutal ocean crossing itself.LOC Slavery would remain legal in the United States for nearly 250 years after that first ship reached Jamestown, until the Thirteenth Amendment abolished it in 1865.

Worth Noticing

Historians are careful to point out that the experience of enslaved Africans isn't really a story of "immigration" at all, since immigration describes people choosing to move. It's a separate and distinct history — one of forced removal, captivity, and survival.LOC

Understanding both of these histories side by side — one of people choosing to cross an ocean for a new life, and one of people being forced across it against their will — is essential to understanding how the United States became a "nation of immigrants" in the first place.

Section 2

The Great Waves

Starting in the 1840s, immigration to the United States changed completely. Instead of a slow trickle of newcomers, huge waves of people began arriving. First came Ireland and Germany, then later immigrants came from all over Europe and Asia. Two famous places tell this story best: Ellis Island in New York Harbor and Angel Island in San Francisco Bay. Both processed millions of immigrants. But they treated the people who passed through them very differently.

Ireland and Germany: fleeing hunger and turmoil

In 1845, a disease began destroying Ireland's potato crop, which most Irish families depended on for food. The crop failed again and again for years. More than 750,000 people in Ireland starved to death.USHistory.org Desperate to survive, over two million Irish people left for the United States during and after this famine.USHistory.org Most arrived with almost no money and settled in crowded East Coast cities, where they took factory and labor jobs.

Around the same time, more than a million Germans immigrated to the United States between 1845 and 1855.USHistory.org Their reasons were different from the Irish. Germany was going through economic hard times and political conflict, including a failed revolution in 1848. Many German immigrants arrived with more money than Irish immigrants did, which let them buy farmland in the Midwest instead of settling only in cities.

Key Word — Famine

An extreme, widespread shortage of food that causes severe hunger and death. The Irish Potato Famine of the 1840s is one of the most well-known famines in history.

Ellis Island: the gateway for millions

As immigration from Europe kept growing, the U.S. government opened a new processing station in New York Harbor. Ellis Island operated from 1892 to 1954, and it became the busiest immigration station in the country.NPS More than 12 million immigrants passed through its halls, most of them from European countries like Italy, Russia, Poland, and Greece.Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation

For most people, Ellis Island meant a health check and a few questions. Most were free within a few hours to start their new lives. Historians estimate that a large majority of arriving immigrants passed through in under a day. Today, roughly 40% of all Americans can trace at least one ancestor back through Ellis Island.Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation

Worth Noticing

Ellis Island wasn't the only entry point, and it wasn't built to keep people out. It was built to process huge numbers of European arrivals quickly. Angel Island, on the other side of the country, worked very differently — as the next section explains.

Angel Island: a very different welcome

While Ellis Island welcomed European immigrants, a different immigration station opened on Angel Island in San Francisco Bay. It operated from 1910 to 1940 and became the main entry point for immigrants arriving from Asia, especially China.Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation

Angel Island was not designed to welcome newcomers quickly the way Ellis Island was. It was built to detain and question them.Angel Island Immigration Station Foundation Chinese immigrants in particular faced long interrogations. Many were held for weeks, months, or even years while officials decided whether to let them in. This harsh treatment was not an accident. It was the result of a law passed decades earlier.

The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882

In 1882, Congress passed the Chinese Exclusion Act. It banned Chinese laborers from immigrating to the United States for ten years. It also required Chinese people already living in the country to carry special identification papers.National Archives The National Archives describes it as the first major U.S. law to restrict immigration based on someone's nationality.National Archives The U.S. State Department's official historians describe it the same way, calling it the first law in American history to place broad restrictions on immigration.State Department, Office of the Historian

This law was exclusionary and discriminatory — it targeted people specifically because of their nationality and ethnicity, not because of anything they had done. Congress kept extending it. The Geary Act of 1892 renewed the exclusion for another ten years. In 1902, lawmakers made Chinese exclusion permanent and expanded it to Hawaii and the Philippines.State Department, Office of the Historian These exclusion laws stayed in effect for 61 years, until Congress repealed them in 1943. By then, the broader pattern of restricting immigration by nationality had already expanded far beyond Chinese immigrants, as the next section explains.

Section 3

The Quota Era

In 1924, Congress didn't just restrict one nationality — it rewrote the rules for almost everyone. A new law created strict numerical limits on immigration, based on where a person came from. It replaced the older, narrower exclusion laws with a much bigger system, one built around a mathematical formula. That formula wasn't neutral. It was calculated specifically to favor some countries and shut out others.

The 1924 Immigration Act

In 1924, Congress passed the Immigration Act of 1924, also known as the Johnson-Reed Act. It set a strict yearly limit on immigration and divided that limit into quotas — a fixed number of visas — for each country.State Department, Office of the Historian Each country's quota was based on how many people from that country already lived in the United States, according to the 1890 census.State Department, Office of the Historian

Choosing the 1890 census was not an accident. Most Southern and Eastern European immigrants — Italians, Greeks, Poles, Russians, and others — arrived in the U.S. after 1890. Using that earlier year meant their communities barely counted toward the new quotas. The State Department's official historians explain what this shift did. Visas for "the British Isles and Western Europe increased," while "newer immigration from other areas like Southern and Eastern Europe was limited."State Department, Office of the Historian

Key Word — National-Origins Quota

A limit on immigration that sets a specific number of visas for people from each country, based on nationality rather than any individual's skills or story.

A formula designed to discriminate

This wasn't a side effect of the law. Favoring Northern and Western Europe was the goal. The State Department's Office of the Historian states plainly that "the most basic purpose of the 1924 Immigration Act was to preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity."State Department, Office of the Historian The National Archives agrees. It describes the same law as one that "barred Asian immigrants, limited Latin American immigrants, and established rigid immigration quotas for European countries." Those quotas heavily favored countries like Germany and Great Britain over countries like Estonia or Latvia.National Archives

Worth Noticing

This is documented historical fact, not one side of a debate. Government sources use direct language here. The law aimed to "preserve" one specific ethnic makeup of the country. Its formula was built to favor some nationalities and restrict others.

The 1924 Act went even further than restricting Southern and Eastern Europe. It excluded people from Asia almost entirely. The law barred "any alien who by virtue of race or nationality was ineligible for citizenship" — a rule that blocked immigration from Japan and the rest of Asia.State Department, Office of the Historian Combined with the older Chinese Exclusion Act, this meant that by the mid-1920s, almost no legal path to the U.S. existed for people from Asia.

A system built to last — for four decades

The national-origins quota system didn't fade away quickly. According to the National Archives, these quotas "lasted from 1924 through 1965." That's more than forty years shaping who could come to America.National Archives Lawmakers adjusted parts of the system over the decades. But the basic idea — a strict quota favoring some nationalities over others — stayed firmly in place.

That finally changed in 1965, when Congress passed a sweeping new law that abolished the national-origins quota system for good. What replaced it, and why, is the subject of the next section.

Section 4

The Modern System Begins

For more than forty years, a person's country of birth decided almost everything about their chances of immigrating to the United States. The national-origins quota system from the last section had favored Northern and Western Europe and had nearly shut out Asia entirely. In 1965, Congress finally tore that formula out and replaced it. What lawmakers built in its place is still, in its basic shape, the system the United States uses today.

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965

On October 3, 1965, President Lyndon B. Johnson signed the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965. He signed it at the base of the Statue of Liberty.National Archives The law is commonly known as the Hart-Celler Act, named after its two main sponsors in Congress, Senator Philip Hart and Representative Emanuel Celler.National Archives, DocsTeach

The law's first job was to get rid of the old system. The National Archives says it eliminated quotas that had "barred Asian immigrants, limited Latin American immigrants, and established rigid immigration quotas for European countries."National Archives A person's nationality no longer decided whether they could come to America.

Key Word — Preference System

A way of ranking immigration applicants using categories like family ties or job skills, instead of using a country-by-country quota.

A new system: family and skills, not nationality

In place of the quotas, the 1965 Act built a preference system. The National Archives explains that it prioritized two things: keeping families together and admitting workers with needed skills.National Archives Close relatives of U.S. citizens — spouses, children, and parents — could immigrate without being counted against any numerical limit at all.National Archives, DocsTeach Beyond those immediate relatives, the law set up ranked categories for other family members and for workers with skills the country needed.

Worth Noticing

This wasn't a small adjustment. It was a complete change in the basic question the government asked. The old system asked, "Where were you born?" The new system asked, "Do you have close family here, or skills this country needs?"

The direct ancestor of today's system

The 1965 Act didn't just matter in its own moment. Its family-and-employment framework is still recognizably the basis of U.S. immigration law today. Researchers who study immigration policy describe today's family-based and employment-based visa categories as growing directly out of the preference system Congress created in 1965.Migration Policy Institute The details have changed over the decades, but the core structure has not.

The next section explains how that system actually works today. It covers how family and employment categories function, and what paths exist for someone hoping to immigrate to the United States now.

Section 5

How Does Immigration Work Today?

The 1965 Act's basic idea — family ties and job skills instead of national origin — is still how U.S. immigration law works today. But "how it works" covers a lot of ground: green cards, citizenship, asylum, refugees, and government agencies with confusing initials. This section walks through the major pieces, using the U.S. government's own official explanations of each one.

Getting a green card: family and jobs

A green card makes someone a lawful permanent resident — allowed to live and work in the United States permanently, without being a citizen. According to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), the federal agency that runs the legal immigration system, most green cards are granted through one of two main paths: family or employment.USCIS

On the family side, USCIS splits applicants into two groups. Immediate relatives of U.S. citizens — spouses, unmarried children under 21, and parents — face no yearly limit on how many green cards can be issued.USCIS Everyone else with a qualifying family relationship falls into a family preference category, ranked F1 through F4, covering relatives like adult children or siblings of citizens. USCIS caps how many preference green cards it issues each year, so people in these categories often wait years for their turn.USCIS

On the employment side, USCIS uses five ranked preference categories, called EB-1 through EB-5. USCIS describes EB-1 as reserved for "persons of extraordinary ability" and outstanding researchers; EB-2 for people with advanced degrees or exceptional ability; EB-3 for skilled and other workers; EB-4 for specific "special immigrant" groups like certain religious workers; and EB-5 for business investors who put at least $800,000 into a new U.S. company that creates jobs.USCIS About 140,000 employment-based green cards are available each year.USCIS

Key Word — Green Card

The common nickname for a Permanent Resident Card. It shows that someone has lawful permanent resident status — the right to live and work in the U.S. permanently, one step short of citizenship.

Becoming a citizen: naturalization

A green card holder can eventually apply to become a U.S. citizen through a process called naturalization. USCIS lays out the general eligibility rules on its own naturalization pages. In most cases, an applicant must have held a green card for at least five years before applying — or three years if they are married to a U.S. citizen.USCIS

During that time, USCIS checks two related things. The applicant needs continuous residence, meaning the U.S. stayed their real home the whole time, without long absences that break that continuity.USCIS The applicant also needs physical presence, meaning they actually spent at least half that time physically inside the United States — at least 30 months out of the standard five years.USCIS

Applicants must also pass an interview that includes an English test and a civics test about U.S. history and government, take an Oath of Allegiance, and meet USCIS's standards of "good moral character."USCIS Only after all of that is an applicant sworn in as a citizen at a naturalization ceremony.

Worth Noticing

Naturalization isn't quick. Between the years of required residence and the paperwork, interview, and testing involved, becoming a U.S. citizen through this process is a multi-year commitment, not a one-time application.

Asylum and refugee status: a legal difference about location

People fleeing danger in their home country sometimes seek protection in the United States through refugee or asylum status. Both use the exact same legal definition: someone who has been persecuted, or has a well-founded fear of future persecution, because of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.USCIS

What separates the two statuses, according to USCIS, is location at the time of application. Refugee status can be granted only to people who are still outside the United States when they apply. Asylum status can be granted only to people who are already physically present in the United States or arriving at a U.S. port of entry when they apply.USCIS Same legal standard, different location — that's the core distinction.

Key Word — Persecution

Serious mistreatment or harm directed at someone because of who they are — their race, religion, nationality, social group, or political beliefs. Fear of persecution is the legal basis for both refugee and asylum status.

What is ICE?

ICE stands for U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement. It is a federal law enforcement agency inside the Department of Homeland Security (DHS).ICE

ICE traces back to the reorganization of the federal government after the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks. Congress passed the Homeland Security Act in November 2002, and DHS formally opened as a new Cabinet-level department on March 1, 2003, combining pieces of 22 different existing federal agencies.DHS ICE itself was created in 2003 as part of that reorganization, formed by merging investigative and enforcement units from the former U.S. Customs Service with the former Immigration and Naturalization Service.ICE

ICE's legal authority is enforcing immigration and customs law within U.S. borders — for example, identifying, arresting, detaining, and removing people who are in the country in violation of immigration law.ICE This is a different agency, with a different job, from U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP). CBP describes its own mission as securing official ports of entry and the border itself — screening travelers and cargo as they arrive in the country.CBP In short: CBP's job is mainly at the border and points of entry; ICE's job is mainly enforcement inside the country's interior.

Key Word — Department of Homeland Security (DHS)

The federal department created in 2003, after the September 11 attacks, to coordinate the country's efforts against terrorism and other security threats. ICE and CBP are both agencies within DHS.
