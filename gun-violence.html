Keeping Schools Safe

Policy, not headlines — what schools and lawmakers have actually tried, how the law is built, and how the United States compares to other countries. Here's what's been done, and what's being debated, as of August 2026.

Student Edition · Updated August 18, 2026
This page aims for strict nonpartisanship — see the Resources section for how it's sourced.
Photo: the west front of the United States Capitol, Washington, D.C. Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

A note before you start: the next paragraphs describe lockdown drills and talk honestly about gun violence and school safety. They don't include graphic details. If you need to pause or talk to someone while reading, that's okay.

The lights go off first. A teacher locks the door, or pushes a desk in front of it. Students move to a corner, out of sight of the windows, and stay quiet. No one talks or checks a phone. Everyone waits for an announcement that it's safe to stand up again. This wasn't always the routine.

If you have done a lockdown drill, you already know this subject from the inside. What you may not know is the shape of the numbers behind it. They are not what most people assume. Somebody counts them, carefully, and it is worth knowing who does the counting and how.

Start with the biggest number. In 2024, the most recent full year of data, 44,447 people in the United States died from a gun injury. That count comes from the CDC, the federal agency that tracks how Americans die.Johns Hopkins, citing CDC Most people guess that number is mostly murders. It is not. The largest share by far was suicide: 27,593 deaths. Homicides came second, at 15,364. If any of this is close to home for you, you can call or text 988 any time, any day, and reach a real person.988lifeline.org

School shootings are a small part of that total and their own serious problem, and they are counted separately. The K-12 School Shooting Database, run by a homeland security researcher, records every time a gun is fired, shown with intent to harm, or a bullet hits school property, going back to 1966. It counted 349 incidents in 2023, the highest year it has on record, and about 231 in 2025.K-12 School Shooting Database Other trackers report different totals for the same years, because they do not all count the same things. That is not anybody being dishonest. It is what happens when people measure something that has no agreed definition. So it is worth asking what a number counts before you repeat it.

One more counting rule matters here. To compare places fairly you need a number called a rateA number adjusted for population size, so places with more or fewer people can be compared fairly. Usually shown as a rate 'per 100,000 people.', not a raw total. A big state will almost always have more of anything than a small state, simply because more people live there. A rate takes population out of the picture. That is why the spread is worth a second look: the highest state's rate is about seven times the lowest one's.

So who decides what to do about any of this? Mostly not schools, and mostly not one place. Individual districts choose things like drills, door locks and whether to have a police officer on campus. States pass their own gun laws and school safety rules, and they differ a lot from each other. Congress can pass federal law but has done so rarely on this subject. Courts then decide whether those laws fit the Second Amendment. So four different sets of people decide, at four different levels. That is a big part of why the answers look so different depending on where you live.

Where things stand, August 2026. The 2023 peak of 349 school incidents has come down for two straight years, to roughly 231 in 2025. But KFF, a nonpartisan health research group, compared how often students were exposed to a school shooting in 1999–2004 with 2020–2024, and the rate nearly tripled, from 19 per 100,000 students to 51.KFF Both of those are true at once, which is the honest state of it: well below the 2023 peak, and well above where it was a generation ago.

A recent example of state-level gun legislation

The California State Capitol in Sacramento, where lawmakers passed Assembly Bill 1127. Wikimedia Commons, Asilvero (CC BY-SA 3.0)

Gun laws change at the state level fairly often. One recent example is California's Assembly Bill 1127. It bars licensed gun dealers from selling semiautomatic pistols that are easy to convert into fully automatic weapons. Those conversions use a small add-on part sometimes called a "switch." California's governor approved the bill on October 10, 2025, and its dealer-sales rule took effect on July 1, 2026.California Legislative Information

This is one example of state-level activity, not a national law — states regularly pass their own gun legislation on many different topics, as Section 4 explains.

A 2023 school-officer law, still unfinished in 2026

Texas House Bill 3, passed in 2023, says each public school campus should have an armed security officer during regular hours. The officer can be a school police officer, a school resource officerA sworn police officer, employed by a local law enforcement agency, who works in a school on a regular or full-time basis., or another commissioned peace officer. Should the U.S. bring back a federal assault weapons ban?

Section 2 covered how the 1994 federal assault weapons ban expired in 2004 and was never renewed. Whether Congress should pass a new one is a genuinely contested question, with real organizations on each side.

Giffords Law Center's stated position

Giffords Law Center, a gun-violence-prevention group, supports a new federal ban. It says these guns are "the weapon of choice" in mass-casualty attacks and cause more severe wounds. It also cites research finding mass-shooting deaths were 70% less likely during the 10 years the earlier ban was in effect.Giffords Law Center

NRA's stated position

The NRA, a gun-rights group, opposes a new federal ban. It cites a government study finding "the banned guns were never used in more than a modest fraction of gun murders." It also argues these guns are legally protected as "in common use" for lawful purposes, the standard set in District of Columbia v. Heller.NRA-ILA

Should 18-to-20-year-olds be able to buy a gun?

Some states set the minimum age to buy certain guns at 21, rather than 18. Whether that kind of law is fair, and whether it's constitutional, is being argued in real court cases.

Giffords Law Center's stated position

Giffords Law Center supports a minimum age of 21 to buy a handgun. It cites brain-development research showing "impulse control, judgement, and long-range planning" may not fully mature "until at least age 26." It also notes 18-to-20-year-olds are "just 4% of the US population, but account for 17% of known gun homicide offenders."Giffords Law Center

NRA's stated position

The NRA, a gun-rights group, is challenging a Florida law barring adults under 21 from buying a gun, arguing it violates the Second Amendment. It says 18-year-olds "are considered adults who can vote, enter into contracts, marry, and enlist and fight for our country" and deserve the same right to self-defense.NRA-ILA

Should more states adopt or expand red-flag laws?

Some states let a court temporarily take away a person's guns if there's evidence they're a danger to themselves or others. Section 4 explains this state-level tool, known as an extreme risk protection orderA civil court order, also called a red-flag law, that can temporarily restrict a person's access to firearms. Different states that have passed this kind of law set their own specific rules for how it works.. Whether more states should pass this kind of law, or broaden who can request one, is contested.

Giffords Law Center's stated position

Giffords Law Center supports wider adoption of these laws, calling them "a proactive way to stop mass shootings and other tragedies" when someone shows "clear warning signs of violence." It also wants more people able to petition a court — noting some states already allow "mental health providers" and "school administrators" to do so, not just police.Giffords Law Center

Crime Prevention Research Center's stated position

The Crime Prevention Research Center, a gun-policy research group, is skeptical of expanding these laws, calling them procedurally flawed. It says a judge often issues the initial order after "a mere written complaint," before the person affected is heard, and that existing involuntary-commitment laws already offer more protections, including "legal representation" and "mental health expert evaluation."Crime Prevention Research Center

1 · Understanding the scale 11 min
Section 1

Understanding the Scale

Before you read 11 min

Lockdown drills can feel normal when you have always had them. They are new.

The numbers below come from federal health data. Some of them are hard to read.

Growing up with lockdown drills might feel normal. It hasn't always been this way. Students today practice hiding from an active shooter the way earlier generations practiced fire drills. That routine is relatively new in American schools. Here's what the numbers show, and how they got this way.

The CDC's headquarters in Atlanta, Georgia — the federal agency that tracks gun deaths nationwide. Wikimedia Commons, CDC/James Gathany (public domain)

How many people die from guns in America each year? The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, or CDCA federal health agency that tracks disease, injury, and death across the United States., tracks this every year. In 2024, the most recent full year of data, 44,447 people in the U.S. died from a gun-related injury.Johns Hopkins Center for Gun Violence Solutions, citing CDC data

That number breaks down into different categories. Most people assume gun deaths are mostly homicides. The data says something different. Gun suicides made up the largest share, at 27,593 deaths. Gun homicides were next, at 15,364 deaths. The remaining 1,490 deaths were law enforcement actions, accidents, or deaths where the cause could not be determined.Johns Hopkins Center for Gun Violence Solutions, citing CDC data Gun suicide has been the largest single category for years, and 2024's total was the highest ever recorded.Johns Hopkins Center for Gun Violence Solutions report (PDF) If you or someone you know is struggling, help is available right now. You can call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time of day, every day of the year.988lifeline.org

How many of these happen at schools?

Most gun deaths do not happen at schools. But school shootings are their own serious problem, and researchers track them closely. The K-12 School Shooting DatabaseA research database, run by homeland security researcher David Riedman, that records every time a gun is fired, shown with intent to harm, or a bullet hits school property in the U.S. since 1966. is one of the most detailed trackers. It records every time a gun is fired, brandished, or a bullet hits school property, going back to 1966.K-12 School Shooting Database (Riedman, 2025)

By this database's count, gun violence at schools hit an all-time high in 2023, with 349 separate incidents.RAND Corporation, citing K-12 School Shooting Database The number dropped after that. In 2025, the same database counted roughly 231 incidents at K-12 schools, with 148 total victims.Omnilert, citing K-12 School Shooting Database

That two-year dip is real, but it doesn't tell the long-run story. KFF, a nonpartisan health policy research group, looked further back. It compared how often students were exposed to a school shooting in 1999-2004 to how often it happened in 2020-2024. The rate nearly tripled, rising from 19 exposures per 100,000 students to 51 per 100,000.KFF Most of that increase happened during the pandemic years. It was not a smooth, steady climb every single year.KFF So the honest picture is not "always going up" and not "always going down." It is a level that has climbed a lot since the late 1990s, with bumps and dips along the way, including the recent one from 2023 to 2025.

Worth Noticing

Different trackers count school shootings differently. Some count any time a gun is fired on school property. Others only count incidents with a certain number of victims. That is why you will see different totals from different sources for the same year — they are not measuring the exact same thing.

Even with 2025's lower count, gun violence at schools remains far more common than it was decades ago.

How does Washington compare to other states?

Gun deaths and school shootings don't happen evenly across the country. Some states have higher numbers than others. Researchers measure this in two different ways, and it's important not to mix them up.

The first way is a rate per capitaA number adjusted for population size, so places with more or fewer people can be fairly compared. Usually shown as a rate 'per 100,000 people.'. This adjusts for how many people live in a state, so a big state and a small state can be fairly compared. The second way is a raw count — just the total number of incidents, with no adjustment for population. A big state will almost always have a higher raw count than a small state, just because it has more people and more schools. Below are both kinds of numbers: a map of gun death rates, and a table of school-shooting counts. They measure different things, so don't expect them to match.

A small state can quietly have the worst rate in the country. It might barely crack the top ten in raw incidents. A huge state can do the opposite. So how did the U.S. get here?

Numbers alone don't explain why gun policy in America looks the way it does. Part of the answer starts with one sentence written in 1791.

2 · The Second Amendment 5 min
Section 2

The Second Amendment & Landmark Laws

Before you read 5 min

Every gun law in America traces back to one sentence added to the Constitution in 1791.

For most of U.S. history courts tied that sentence to militia service. That reading changed in 2008, and the change is why the argument looks the way it does now.

So how did the U.S. end up here? Every gun law in America connects back to one sentence. That sentence is the Second Amendment. It was added to the U.S. Constitution in 1791. It reads: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed."National Archives That's the whole amendment. It's just one sentence. But people have argued about what it means for over 200 years.

The U.S. Capitol, where Congress has passed every major federal gun law in this story. Wikimedia Commons, Architect of the Capitol (public domain)

What the Supreme Court decided in 2008

For most of American history, courts mostly connected this amendment to militia service. A militiaAn armed group of ordinary citizens, not full-time soldiers, that a state could call on to defend itself. is a group of citizens who can be called on to defend their state. But in 2008, the Supreme Court took a major case. It was called District of Columbia v. Heller.

The Court ruled 5-4. It held that the Second Amendment protects an individual person's right to own a gun. That right applies even if the person is not part of a militia. The Court said people can keep a gun at home for "traditionally lawful purposes," including self-defense.Cornell Law School (Supreme Court syllabus) This was a major shift. It was the first time the Court had ruled this way. The case struck down a strict handgun ban in Washington, D.C.Cornell Law School (Supreme Court syllabus)

Worth Noticing

Heller settled one big question. It said the Second Amendment protects an individual right. But it did not settle every question about gun laws. Courts are still deciding how far that right goes, and which gun laws can still stand.

Four laws that shaped today's rules

Congress has passed several major gun laws since the 1930s. Here are four of the most important ones.

The National Firearms Act (1934) was the first major federal gun law. It taxed and required registration for the most dangerous weapons. That includes machine guns, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and silencersA device attached to a gun that muffles the sound it makes when fired.. Congress passed it during an era of gangster violence. Owners of these weapons still have to register them with the federal government today.

The Gun Control Act (1968) went much further. It created the system of federally licensed dealersA business or person the federal government has approved to sell guns legally, after passing a background check and other requirements. that still exists today. Anyone who sells guns as a business must get a federal license. The law also created "prohibited person" categories. Convicted felons, for example, cannot legally buy a gun.

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act (1993) added a new requirement. Licensed dealers must run a background checkA check of criminal and other records, run before someone can buy a gun from a licensed dealer, to see if they are legally allowed to own one. before selling a gun. This stops many prohibited buyers before a sale happens. A national instant-check system runs these checks in minutes.Wikipedia, citing Pub. L. 103-159

James Brady was shot in the head and left paralyzed in the 1981 attempt on President Reagan's life. He and his wife Sarah spent the next twelve years pushing Congress for this law. "If it hadn't been for them, we would not have passed the Brady law," President Clinton said when he signed it.

James Brady, 1929–2014 · See Key People, below

The federal assault weapons ban (1994) banned certain semi-automatic weapons and large ammunition magazines. But the law had a built-in expiration date. It was written to last only ten years. Congress did not pass a new law to extend it. So the ban expired on its own in 2004, and it has not been renewed since.Wikipedia, citing Pub. L. 103-322

These four laws are still the foundation of federal gun policy today. But none of them were written specifically for schools. That gap is part of why schools have had to decide for themselves what to try.

3 · Safety measures tried 16 min
Section 3

School Safety Measures That Have Been Tried

Before you read 16 min

There is no single national rule for school safety, so districts have each tried their own mix — cameras, officers, drills, detectors.

For most of these, researchers have looked at whether they work, and the honest answer is often "we do not know yet."

First: Understanding the Scale

With no single federal rule for schools, they have tried a lot of things on their own. After a shooting makes the news, districts often respond fast. They add cameras, hire officers, run drills, or lock down entrances. By the 2021-22 school year some of these measures were in almost every school. Others were rare. And researchers have studied many of them directly. Here's what's actually been tried, how common each measure is, and what the evidence says about whether it works.

A walk-through metal detector installed at a school entrance. Wikimedia Commons (CC0)

Metal detectors

A metal detectorA device that senses metal objects, like guns or knives, as a person walks through it or is scanned with a handheld wand. is a device that senses metal on a person's body. Some schools use walk-through models at entrances. Others use handheld wands for random checks. Despite the attention metal detectors get after a shooting, they are actually one of the least common school safety measures. In the 2021-22 school year, only 6 percent of U.S. public schools used random metal detector checks on students, and even fewer used daily checks.National Center for Education Statistics, School Survey on Crime and Safety, 2021-22

Researchers have looked closely at whether metal detectors actually work. A 2019 research brief from WestEd's Justice & Prevention Research Center reviewed decades of studies. It found "little evidence to support their effectiveness at preventing school shootings or successfully detecting weapons at schools."Schildkraut & Grogan, WestEd Justice & Prevention Research Center (2019) The same brief found something else, too. Students in schools with metal detectors reported feeling less safe, not more, than students in schools without them.Schildkraut & Grogan, WestEd Justice & Prevention Research Center (2019) Researchers have also found that metal detectors show up more often in schools with larger shares of Black and Latino students. That held true even after accounting for how much crime happens nearby.Schildkraut & Grogan, WestEd Justice & Prevention Research Center (2019)

School resource officers

A school resource officerA sworn police officer, employed by a local law enforcement agency, who works in a school on a regular or full-time basis., or SRO, is a police officer assigned to work inside a school. This is one of the most common security measures. By the 2019-20 school year, 65 percent of U.S. public schools reported having security staff present at least once a week, up from 43 percent a decade earlier. That figure dipped slightly to 61 percent in 2021-22.National Center for Education Statistics, School Survey on Crime and Safety

SROs are also one of the most studied — and most debated — measures on this list. Research findings genuinely point in different directions, depending on what outcome is being measured.

Some rigorous studies have found safety benefits. A 2017 study looked at a federal grant program that paid to place SROs in schools. It found the funding was linked to more officially recorded weapons and drug crimes being caught at school — a sign that SROs may improve detection of dangerous items.Owens (2017), cited in RAND Corporation (2024) A 2021 study of middle schools in North Carolina found that SRO presence was linked to fewer reports of serious violent behavior.Sorensen, Shen & Bushway (2021), cited in RAND Corporation (2024) A larger 2023 study used national data from nearly 95,000 schools. It found that schools with SROs had 30 percent fewer non-firearm violent incidents, like fights and threats, than schools without them.Sorensen et al. (2023), cited in RAND Corporation (2024)

Other research points to real costs. A major 2023 meta-analysisA study that combines and analyzes the results of many earlier studies on the same question, to look for overall patterns. found consistently strong evidence linking SROs to more suspensions, expulsions, and law-enforcement referrals. That effect was largest for Black and Latino students.Fisher et al. (2023), cited in RAND Corporation (2024) A federal study published in 2022 found something more specific. SROs were linked to fewer suspensions overall, and fewer suspensions of Black students specifically — but researchers did not find that same drop for white students.Fisher, Dawson-Edwards, Swartz, Higgins, Coffey & Overstreet, National Institute of Justice (2022) For scale: in 2017-18, Black students made up about 15 percent of public school enrollment nationally. But they made up about 32 percent of school-related arrests.RAND Corporation, citing U.S. Department of Education Civil Rights Data Collection (2024)

Worth Noticing

Notice that these findings aren't contradicting each other by accident. They're measuring different things. Some studies ask "does this catch more weapons or reduce violent incidents?" Others ask "does this change how often students get suspended, expelled, or arrested?" A measure can do both at once — that's exactly what the research on SROs has found.

Lockdown drills

A lockdown drillA practice exercise where students and staff act out what they would do if a dangerous person were in or near the school, often by hiding, locking doors, and staying silent. is a practice exercise for responding to a threat inside or near the school. These drills were close to universal by the 2021-22 school year, when about 96 percent of U.S. public schools had drilled students on lockdown procedures.National Center for Education Statistics, School Survey on Crime and Safety, 2021-22

Researchers have started studying what these drills do to students' mental health. A 2023 study surveyed children who came to a pediatric emergency room, along with their caregivers. It found that 27 percent of children reported anxiety after a lockdown drill. Children who had a history of being bullied, or of skipping school out of fear, were more likely to report that anxiety.Riggs et al., Journal of School Health (2023) The same study found that many caregivers didn't recognize when their child was struggling afterward. Caregivers and children often disagreed about whether the child felt anxious or down after a drill.Riggs et al., Journal of School Health (2023) The study's authors concluded that lockdown drills may increase mental health symptoms in children. They said more research is needed to find out which students are most affected.Riggs et al., Journal of School Health (2023)

Threat assessment programs

A threat assessment programA school team, usually including an administrator, a counselor, and a school resource officer, that meets to evaluate specific reports of concerning behavior and decide how to respond. works differently than the other measures on this list. It's not a device or a guard. It's a process. A trained team reviews reports of concerning student behavior. That team is usually a mix of administrators, counselors, and school resource officers. The team decides how serious a risk is, then creates a plan to support that student and address the risk. The goal is to step in before a threat turns into violence, not just to react after something happens.

Adoption has grown fast. A decade ago, fewer than half of U.S. schools had a threat assessment team. By April 2024, roughly eight in ten schools reported having one.RAND Corporation & U.S. Secret Service National Threat Assessment Center (2025), citing NCES School Pulse Panel

A 2025 national survey of principals, run by the Secret Service's National Threat Assessment Center, found most of these teams rely on support rather than punishment. Over 75 percent of principals said they rarely or never use suspension or expulsion for students referred to their threat assessment team. About 80 percent said they rarely or never involve arrest or prosecution.RAND Corporation & U.S. Secret Service National Threat Assessment Center (2025) A separate study looked at one widely used model, the Comprehensive School Threat Assessment Guidelines. It found that students who went through the program were less likely to be suspended, expelled, or arrested than students who didn't.Overview and Research Summary of the Comprehensive School Threat Assessment Guidelines (CSTAG)

Security vestibules and access control

A security vestibuleA small, secured entryway — often with locked doors on both sides and a window for checking ID — that visitors must pass through before reaching the rest of the school. is a controlled entry area. Visitors are stopped and checked before they can reach classrooms or hallways. This was one of the most widely adopted school safety measures in the country by the 2021-22 school year, when 97 percent of U.S. public schools reported controlling access to their buildings during school hours.National Center for Education Statistics, School Survey on Crime and Safety, 2021-22 Security cameras have grown just as fast — used by 93 percent of schools in 2021-22, up from 61 percent in 2009-10.National Center for Education Statistics, School Survey on Crime and Safety

Because these measures were already that common by 2021-22, researchers have a harder time comparing schools that have them to schools that don't. Almost every school does. Security experts generally agree on one thing, though. A single unlocked or propped-open door can defeat the purpose of a vestibule entirely. So how consistently a school follows its own procedure matters as much as whether it has the equipment.

School-climate and bullying-reduction programs

Not every measure on this list is about hardware or officers. Many schools have also invested in programs meant to improve the overall climate of the school. Three come up most often:

  • social-emotional learningClassroom lessons that teach students skills like managing emotions, resolving conflict, and communicating — the idea is that stronger social skills reduce the conditions that lead to violence.
  • restorative justiceAn approach to discipline that focuses on repairing harm and rebuilding relationships, instead of relying only on suspension or punishment.
  • trauma-informed careAn approach to working with students that assumes difficult behavior may be connected to past trauma, and responds with support rather than only punishment.

For years, many researchers believed that reducing bullying and improving school climate would also reduce school shootings, since many attackers had histories of being bullied or feeling isolated.KQED Mindshift (July 2026)

A Researcher Changes His Mind

In April 2026, one of the country's leading school-violence researchers said that theory doesn't hold up. Ron Avi Astor is a UCLA professor who has spent decades studying school violence. He told fellow researchers at the American Educational Research Association's annual meeting: "We were wrong." For years, Astor believed that improving school climate and reducing bullying would help prevent school shootings.KQED Mindshift (July 2026)

He changed his mind after comparing two graphs covering 2001 to 2024. One tracked California students who said they carried a weapon to school. That number went down substantially over those years. The other tracked school shootings nationwide. That number went up sharply over the same period. If bullying and school climate were the main driver, Astor said, those two lines should have moved together. They didn't.KQED Mindshift (July 2026)

Astor's revised view is that most school shooters are driven less by local bullying. He now thinks they're driven more by a desire for notoriety. "Their number one audience was the media," he said. "That's really clear to me now."KQED Mindshift (July 2026) Based on this, Astor now recommends media guidelines. Those guidelines would limit coverage of a shooter's name and image, and delay the release of any manifestos or videos the attacker leaves behind. He compares this to research on suicide and terrorism. In both areas, researchers have found that heavy publicity can inspire copycat attacks.KQED Mindshift (July 2026)

It's worth being clear about what this reversal is — and isn't. It's one leading researcher, changing his own view based on new evidence, as of April 2026. It is not a final, settled answer for the whole field. Other researchers continue to study school climate, bullying, and violence prevention. Social-emotional learning and restorative justice programs remain widely used for other reasons too, including everyday student well-being and discipline. But Astor's reversal is itself a notable moment. It's a reminder that even well-established theories in this field can change when researchers look hard at new data.

Taken together, these measures show a pattern. The most common ones — locked entrances, cameras, drills — are also the ones researchers have studied the least for stopping an actual shooting. The measures researchers have studied most closely, like SROs and threat assessment, show real but complicated results. There are some clear benefits. There are also real costs that fall unevenly across different groups of students. No single measure has been shown to reliably prevent a school shooting on its own. And every measure a school picks still has to fit inside the gun laws set above it.

4 · How policy is built 7 min
Section 4

How Policy Is Built

Before you read 7 min

Gun rules are not made in one place. Some come from Congress, some from each state.

That split is why the law can look completely different depending on which state you are standing in.

Those laws come from a system bigger than any one school. Gun policy in the U.S. isn't made in just one place. Some rules come from Washington, D.C. Other rules come from each state's own capital. Understanding gun policy means understanding how that split works.

The Colorado Court of Appeals courtroom in Denver — an example of a state-level court, where disputes over state gun laws are often decided. Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)

What the federal government regulates

The federal government sets a baseline set of rules. Every state has to follow these rules, no matter where you live. Two federal laws form the core of this baseline. They are the National Firearms Act of 1934 and the Gun Control Act of 1968. Both laws were covered in Section 2.

These federal laws cover several things. They cover who can sell guns as a business. They cover which buyers must pass a background checkA check of criminal and other records, run before someone can buy a gun from a licensed dealer, to see if they are legally allowed to own one.. And they cover which categories of people are barred from owning a gun at all — such as convicted felons. The federal government's power to make these rules comes mainly from its constitutional authority over interstate commerce.Congressional Research Service, "U.S. Gun Policy: Framework and Major Issues" (2025)

What states regulate

Federal law is described as a "floor," not a ceiling. States are free to add their own, stricter rules on top of it. States just cannot pass a law that directly conflicts with federal law.Congressional Research Service, "U.S. Gun Policy: Framework and Major Issues" (2025)

This is why gun laws can look very different depending on which state you're in. One state might require a permit to carry a concealed gun in public. A neighboring state might not. One state might set its own waiting period before a gun sale. Another might not. These are all choices made at the state level, layered on top of the shared federal baseline.Congressional Research Service, "U.S. Gun Policy: Framework and Major Issues" (2025)

Worth Noticing

Drive across a state line, and the gun laws that apply to you can change instantly. A required permit, a waiting period, a background-check rule — any of it can vanish or appear the moment you cross the border. That's not a glitch. It's the federal floor, with each state building whatever it wants on top.

Red-flag laws: one example of a state-level tool

One example of a state-level policy is a type of law often called a extreme risk protection orderA civil court order, also called a red-flag law, that can temporarily restrict a person's access to firearms. Different states that have passed this kind of law set their own specific rules for how it works., or ERPO. These are sometimes nicknamed "red-flag laws." As of 2019, at least 17 states and Washington, D.C. had passed some version of this kind of law. More states have passed their own versions since then.Congressional Research Service, "Firearm 'Red Flag' Laws" (2019)

An ERPO is a civil court order. That means it is handled in civil court, not criminal court. Details vary by state, but most of these laws share a common structure.

Who can ask a court for one. Only certain people are allowed to petition, or formally ask, a court for this kind of order. In most states, that group is limited to law enforcement officers and, in many cases, family or household members.Congressional Research Service, "Firearm 'Red Flag' Laws" (2019)

What the court can order. A judge can issue two different kinds of orders. The first is a short, temporary order. A judge can issue this kind without the other person present. This happens if the judge finds reasonable cause to believe that person poses an immediate risk. This kind of order usually lasts only a few days to a few weeks. The second is a longer, final order. Before a judge can issue this kind, the other person must be notified. That person must also be given a chance to appear in court. A final order usually requires a higher standard of proof — clear and convincing evidence of a significant danger. If a court grants either kind of order, the named person must turn over any firearms they have. They must do this right away or within a set window of time.Congressional Research Service, "Firearm 'Red Flag' Laws" (2019)

How long it lasts. A final order can last up to one year in most states that have this kind of law. Courts can renew a final order before it expires, following the same kind of court process used to issue it the first time.Congressional Research Service, "Firearm 'Red Flag' Laws" (2019)

Worth Noticing

Extreme risk protection orders work as a general category of state policy tool. Each state that has passed one writes its own specific version, with its own exact rules. Washington's version is in Washington's Story, later.

Whether a state has passed an ERPO law, and exactly how that state's version works, is a decision made entirely at the state level. That's true of most of the policy differences you'll see from state to state — they trace back to this same federal/state structure.

Washington's story 7 min
Close to Home

Washington's School Safety Story

Before you read 7 min

Washington voters wrote one of these laws themselves, approving Initiative 1491 in November 2016.

It created a court process for temporarily removing guns from someone a judge finds to be a danger.

First: How Policy Is Built

Section 4 explained how extreme risk protection orders work as a general category of state law. Washington is one of the states that passed one. Here's how Washington's own version works, plus two other Washington-specific policies connected to school safety: state funding for school security upgrades, and the state's minimum age to buy certain guns.

The Legislative Building at the Washington State Capitol in Olympia, where the state's extreme risk protection order law, school-security funding, and firearm purchase-age law were all passed. Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

Washington's extreme risk protection order law

Washington voters created this law themselves, at the ballot box. On November 8, 2016, voters approved Initiative 1491, which created Washington's extreme risk protection orderA civil court order, also called a red-flag law, that can temporarily restrict a person's access to firearms. Different states that have passed this kind of law set their own specific rules for how it works. process. It took effect the following year.Washington State Legislature, RCW Chapter 7.105

The law has since been recodified — moved into a different, reorganized part of Washington's law books — but the same basic process still applies today. Here's how it works in Washington specifically.

Who can petition. Three types of people can ask a Washington court for an extreme risk protection order. They are an intimate partner, a family or household member, or a law enforcement agency.Washington State Legislature, RCW 7.105.410

What a Washington court can order. A judge can issue a temporary order right away, without the other person present. To do that, the judge must find reasonable cause to believe that person poses a significant danger of injuring themselves or others by having a gun. That temporary order lasts until the full court hearing. At the full hearing, a judge decides whether to issue a full order. Washington law sets the standard of proof as a "preponderance of the evidence" — meaning it's more likely than not to be true. To meet that standard, the judge must find the person poses a significant danger. That danger must come from hurting themselves or others by having, buying, or trying to buy a gun.Washington State Legislature, RCW 7.105.225 If a court issues either kind of order, the named person must surrender any firearms right away. They must also surrender any concealed pistol license they hold.Washington State Legislature, RCW 7.105.330Washington State Legislature, RCW 7.105.335

How long it lasts. A full order in Washington lasts up to one year. Before it expires, the petitioner can request a renewal. The petitioner is the intimate partner, family or household member, or law enforcement agency who asked for it. If a court finds the same standard is still met, it renews the order for one more year. There's no limit on how many times an order can be renewed, as long as a court keeps finding the standard is met.Washington State Legislature, RCW 7.105.410

Worth Noticing

This is the same general tool described in Section 4 — a civil court order, not a criminal charge — just with Washington's own specific rules for who can ask, what a judge must find, and how long it lasts.

State funding for school security upgrades

On May 19, 2025, Washington Governor Bob Ferguson signed Senate Bill 5004, sometimes called "Alyssa's Law," after Alyssa Alhadeff, a student killed in the 2018 shooting at Marjory Stoneman Douglas High School in Parkland, Florida. The Washington Senate passed it 49-0, and the House passed it 98-0. It took effect on July 27, 2025.Washington State Legislature, SB 5004 bill history

The law requires Washington school districts to build an emergency response system that can get help to a school fast. Each district must include at least one of these: a panic or alert button, a live video or audio feed police and school staff can access, remote-control access to doors, or live two-way communication during an emergency.Washington State Legislature, SB 5004, engrossed substitute bill text

To help districts pay for this, the Washington Legislature created the School Security and Preparedness Infrastructure Grant, administered by the Office of Superintendent of Public Instruction (OSPI). As of the 2025-27 capital budget, the Legislature set aside $6 million for this grant program, with no district able to receive more than $500,000.Office of Superintendent of Public Instruction, "2025 Legislative Session in Review" (June 5, 2025) Districts can use the money for things like entrance and door security, including panic buttons, fencing, and other upgrades to expedite a law enforcement response to a threat.Office of Superintendent of Public Instruction, "2025 Legislative Session in Review" (June 5, 2025)

Washington's firearm purchase-age requirement

Washington also has its own state law on the minimum age to buy certain guns — separate from any federal rule. Under Washington law, a person under 21 years old may not purchase a pistol or a semiautomatic assault rifle, and no one may sell or transfer a semiautomatic assault rifle to a person under 21. This rule traces back to Initiative 1639, which Washington voters approved on November 6, 2018.Washington State Legislature, RCW 9.41.240

The law does allow some exceptions to possession — not purchase — for 18-to-20-year-olds. For example, someone in that age range can still possess a semiautomatic assault rifle at their own home, at their fixed place of business, or on property they control, as long as other conditions in the law are met.Washington State Legislature, RCW 9.41.240

Together, these three policies — the extreme risk protection order process, state funding for school security infrastructure, and the purchase-age rule — are all specifically Washington's own. Each one shows how a state builds its own, more detailed version of a general policy tool described in 4 · How policy is built. Washington made these choices within the U.S. system. Other countries have made very different choices.

How other countries handle this 12 min
Global Comparison

How Other Countries Handle This

Before you read 12 min

Every country decides its own gun rules, shaped by its own history.

Australia after 1996 and a few others are worth knowing about, though a policy that fits one country does not automatically fit another.

The United States is not the only country that has to decide how to regulate guns. Every country makes its own choice, based on its own history, culture, and laws. Comparing those choices shows just how wide the range of options really is. The four countries below — Australia, Japan, Switzerland, and Canada — are described by what each one's actual law says. That is not an argument that any one of these approaches is better or worse than another, or better or worse than the U.S. approach above. It's simply a factual look at how gun regulation can differ from country to country.

Australia: a nationwide buyback after a mass shooting

Parliament House in Canberra, where Australia's federal government agreed to the National Firearms Agreement in 1996. Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

On April 28, 1996, a gunman killed 35 people and wounded 18 others at Port Arthur, a historic site in the Australian state of Tasmania. Twelve days later, Australia's federal government and all six of its states and two territories agreed to a single, nationwide set of gun laws called the National Firearms Agreement.RAND Corporation, "The Effects of the 1996 National Firearms Agreement in Australia"

The agreement did several things at once. It banned private ownership, sale, and import of self-loading (semi-automatic) rifles and shotguns, and pump-action shotguns, with narrow exceptions for some farmers and professional shooters. It required a "genuine reason" to get a license to own any gun. Reasons like hunting, pest control on a farm, or membership in an approved shooting club counted. Personal self-defense did not. The agreement also created a national system for registering every gun and set uniform, strict rules for how guns must be stored. To get the newly banned guns out of circulation, the government ran a temporary "buyback" program. Gun owners had a set window of time to turn in now-illegal firearms in exchange for a government payment at market value.RAND Corporation, "The Effects of the 1996 National Firearms Agreement in Australia"

The buyback worked at large scale. Between October 1996 and August 2001, the government bought back 659,940 newly banned firearms. A second, smaller buyback in 2003 collected another 68,727 handguns.RAND Corporation, "The Effects of the 1996 National Firearms Agreement in Australia" Household gun ownership in Australia fell sharply in the years after: one international survey found it dropped from about 15% of households in 1995-1998 to about 6% in 2004-2005.RAND Corporation, "The Effects of the 1996 National Firearms Agreement in Australia"

Worth Noticing

Australia's gun deaths were already falling before 1996 — that trend didn't start with this law. RAND, a nonpartisan research organization, reviewed the studies on what happened next and found the strongest evidence supports a real drop in firearm suicides, mass shootings, and homicides of women specifically, following the agreement. But RAND also found the research questions whether some of those changes can be fully credited to the law itself, versus other factors already in motion at the same time. Both of those things are part of the honest picture.

Japan: near-total restriction, with a real but narrow licensing path

The National Diet Building in Tokyo, seat of Japan's national legislature, which passed the Law Controlling the Possession of Firearms and Swords. Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

Japan's civilian gun law is one of the strictest in the world. But "near-total restriction" is not the same as "completely banned." Under Japan's Law Controlling the Possession of Firearms and Swords, handguns are not available to ordinary civilians at all. The only exception in the law is for researchers using one for testing purposes. But hunting rifles, hunting shotguns, and air guns can legally be owned by civilians who complete a specific licensing process.Law Library of Congress, "Firearms-Control Legislation and Policy"

That process is long. An applicant must take a class on gun laws, safety, and storage. They must also pass a skills test or complete shooting classes. They must also submit documents to the local Public Safety Commission, including a medical certificate. The law lists specific reasons someone can be disqualified. These include a mental illness or dementia that affects full mental ability. They also include addiction to alcohol or drugs, a recent criminal record, or a history of domestic violence or stalking warnings.Law Library of Congress, "Firearms-Control Legislation and Policy"

A rifle license has an even higher bar. It generally isn't granted unless the applicant meets one of three conditions. The applicant must be a professional hunter, or need it for pest control tied to their business. Or the applicant must have already held a hunting-gun license for at least ten years. Once granted, a license lasts three years and can be renewed. The gun itself must be stored in a locker that meets government standards, and police can inspect it.Law Library of Congress, "Firearms-Control Legislation and Policy"

The result of this system is a very small number of legally owned guns for a country of Japan's size. As of 2011, the most recent year with detailed figures in this review, Japan's National Police Agency counted about 271,100 total civilian-owned guns nationwide — nearly all of them hunting rifles, shotguns, or air guns, and none of them handguns.Law Library of Congress, "Firearms-Control Legislation and Policy," citing National Police Agency data

Switzerland: high ownership tied to a citizen-soldier military system

The Bundeshaus (Federal Palace) in Bern, home to the Swiss Parliament, which voted in 2007 to change how militia rifle ammunition is stored. Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)

Switzerland is often described as having both a militiaAn armed group of ordinary citizens, not full-time soldiers, that a state could call on to defend itself.-based military and a high rate of civilian gun ownership, and that connection is real — but it's more specific than it's sometimes made to sound. Military service is compulsory for able-bodied Swiss men. During their years of service, militia members are issued a personal weapon and are generally allowed to keep it at their own home rather than in a government armory.Law Library of Congress, "Firearms-Control Legislation and Policy" When a soldier finishes their service, Swiss law allows them to keep that issued weapon.Law Library of Congress, "Firearms-Control Legislation and Policy"

But that militia system is a separate track from Switzerland's general civilian gun law, the Weapons Act. Under that law, most rifles and shotguns commonly used for hunting don't require an acquisition license to buy from a private individual. A license is required to buy a handgun, or to buy any gun from a licensed dealer. To get one, an applicant must be at least 18, cannot be under legal guardianship, and cannot have a criminal record involving violent crime.Law Library of Congress, "Firearms-Control Legislation and Policy"

The military side of this system has also changed over time in response to safety concerns. Ammunition for the issued militia rifle used to be stored at the soldier's home alongside the weapon. After a string of suicides and domestic killings involving that ammunition, the Swiss Parliament voted in 2007 to require that ammunition be stored in central military arsenals instead. The rifle itself could still generally stay at home.SWI swissinfo.ch (Swiss Broadcasting Corporation), "Soldiers can keep guns at home but not ammo" As of late 2025, Swiss lawmakers were still actively debating whether to reverse that 2007 change. Some wanted to let soldiers bring ammunition home again, citing regional security concerns in Europe. That debate is a sign this specific rule is still contested inside Switzerland's own political system, not a settled fact frozen in time.Le News, "Swiss soldiers may soon take ammunition home again"

Canada: a three-tier "middle ground" system

Centre Block and the Peace Tower on Parliament Hill in Ottawa, home to Canada's Parliament, which has amended the country's list of prohibited firearms several times, most recently in 2025. Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)

Canada's approach is often described as a middle ground between the U.S. and countries with tighter restrictions, and its actual legal structure supports that description. Canada's Criminal Code sorts every firearm into one of three categories. Non-restricted firearms include ordinary hunting rifles and shotguns. Restricted firearms include most handguns and some semi-automatic long guns. Prohibited firearms include fully automatic weapons, sawed-off shotguns, and certain military-style rifles named specifically in the law.Public Safety Canada, "Strengthened Measures to Protect Canadians from Gun Violence"

To legally possess or acquire any firearm in Canada, a person must hold a Possession and Acquisition Licence, or PAL, issued by the Royal Canadian Mounted Police. Getting a PAL requires being at least 18 years old, passing the Canadian Firearms Safety Course, and passing a background checkA check of criminal and other records, run before someone can buy a gun from a licensed dealer, to see if they are legally allowed to own one.. To possess or acquire a restricted firearm, such as most handguns, an applicant needs an additional license level. That level requires also passing the Canadian Restricted Firearms Safety Course. Restricted and prohibited firearms also come with extra rules on where they can be legally stored and transported. Minors aged 12 to 17 can get a limited minor's license to use a non-restricted rifle or shotgun, but only under a licensed adult's responsibility.Royal Canadian Mounted Police, "Apply for a firearms licence"

Canada's list of prohibited firearms has also changed over time through amendments to the law, most recently in 2020, 2024, and 2025, when the government added more than 300 additional makes and models of semi-automatic firearms to the prohibited category. As of that most recent update, the government reported that more than 19,000 non-restricted makes and models — over 127,000 individual firearm variations — remained legally available in Canada for hunting and sport shooting.Public Safety Canada, "Government of Canada prohibits additional assault-style firearms" (2025)

Worth Noticing

These four countries show four different structures. Australia used a nationwide ban and buyback. Japan uses near-total restriction with a narrow licensing path. Switzerland ties part of its system to a citizen-soldier military tradition. Canada uses a three-tier classification system. None of these laws could simply be copied onto the U.S. without also changing the U.S. Constitution, since the Second Amendment limits what gun laws Congress and the states can pass here. The point is to show how other countries have answered the same basic question, not to argue that the U.S. should or shouldn't follow any of them.

1791

The Second Amendment is ratified

The U.S. Capitol, where Congress has passed every major federal gun law on this timeline. Wikimedia Commons, Architect of the Capitol (public domain)

The Bill of Rights, including the Second Amendment, was ratified on December 15, 1791. Its single sentence — "A well regulated MilitiaAn armed group of ordinary citizens, not full-time soldiers, that a state could call on to defend itself., being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed" — is the starting point for every gun law that followed.

1934

The National Firearms Act

Congress passed the first major federal gun law during an era of gangster violence. It taxed and required registration for the most dangerous weapons of the time — machine guns, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and silencersA device attached to a gun that muffles the sound it makes when fired..

→ Wikipedia: National Firearms Act
1968

The Gun Control Act

This law created the system of federally licensed dealersA business or person the federal government has approved to sell guns legally, after passing a background check and other requirements. that still exists today, and it created "prohibited person" categories — including convicted felons — who cannot legally buy a gun.

→ Congressional Research Service: U.S. Gun Policy: Framework and Major Issues
Nov 29, 1990

The Gun-Free School Zones Act

Sen. Herb Kohl of Wisconsin, whose official Senate portrait is held by the U.S. Congress Biographical Directory. Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Sen. Herb Kohl (D-WI) introduced this bill in the Senate on October 27, 1990. It made it a federal crime to knowingly possess a gun in or near a school zone. The Senate and House both passed it the same day, and President George H.W. Bush signed it into law a month later. It's still on the books today, though the Supreme Court forced Congress to rewrite part of it after United States v. Lopez (1995) found the original version exceeded Congress's power to regulate interstate commerce.

→ Cornell Law School: 18 U.S.C. § 922(q), the Gun-Free School Zones Act
1993

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act

James Brady, photographed in 1986, five years after being shot during the 1981 assassination attempt on President Reagan. Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

This law required licensed dealers to run a background checkA check of criminal and other records, run before someone can buy a gun from a licensed dealer, to see if they are legally allowed to own one. before selling a gun. It's named for James Brady, Reagan's press secretary, who was shot and permanently disabled in the March 1981 assassination attempt. He and his wife Sarah spent over a decade pushing for the law that bears his name. President Clinton signed it on November 30, 1993, with the Bradys as his guests of honor.

→ Wikipedia, citing Pub. L. 103-159
1994

The federal assault weapons ban — and rising SRO funding

Congress banned certain semi-automatic weapons and large-capacity magazines, but wrote the ban to expire after ten years. That same year, the newly created federal Office of Community Oriented Policing Services (COPS) began funding local police departments to place more officers, and eventually school resource officers, in communities.

→ Wikipedia, citing Pub. L. 103-322
Apr 20, 1999

Columbine, and a surge in federal SRO funding

The Columbine High School shooting in Colorado is widely seen as a turning point for how Americans think about school safety. That same year, the COPS office started a dedicated COPS in Schools grant program to help districts hire and train school resource officers. Between 1999 and 2008, it awarded $750 million to local police departments for that purpose. Researchers say that period drastically increased how common SROs became in American schools. (Section 3 covers what SROs actually do, and what the research says about them, in detail.)

→ RAND Corporation: School Resource Officers
2004

The assault weapons ban expires

Congress did not pass a new law to extend the 1994 assault weapons ban before its built-in ten-year expiration date. It expired on its own and has not been renewed since.

→ Wikipedia, citing Pub. L. 103-322

District of Columbia v. Heller

The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that the Second Amendment protects an individual person's right to own a gun, even outside militia service, and struck down a strict Washington, D.C. handgun ban. It was the first time the Court had ruled this way.
