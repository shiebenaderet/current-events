The Second Amendment & Landmark Laws

Every gun law in America connects back to one sentence. That sentence is the Second Amendment. It was added to the U.S. Constitution in 1791. It reads: "A well regulated Militia, being necessary to the security of a free State, the right of the people to keep and bear Arms, shall not be infringed."National Archives That's the whole amendment. It's just one sentence. But people have argued about what it means for over 200 years.

What the Supreme Court decided in 2008

For most of American history, courts mostly connected this amendment to militia service. A militia is a group of citizens who can be called on to defend their state. But in 2008, the Supreme Court took a major case. It was called District of Columbia v. Heller.

The Court ruled 5-4. It held that the Second Amendment protects an individual person's right to own a gun. That right applies even if the person is not part of a militia. The Court said people can keep a gun at home for "traditionally lawful purposes," including self-defense.Cornell Law School (Supreme Court syllabus) This was a major shift. It was the first time the Court had ruled this way. The case struck down a strict handgun ban in Washington, D.C.Cornell Law School (Supreme Court syllabus)

Worth Noticing

Heller settled one big question. It said the Second Amendment protects an individual right. But it did not settle every question about gun laws. Courts are still deciding how far that right goes, and which gun laws can still stand.

Four laws that shaped today's rules

Congress has passed several major gun laws since the 1930s. Here are four of the most important ones.

The National Firearms Act (1934) was the first major federal gun law. It taxed and required registration for the most dangerous weapons. That includes machine guns, short-barreled rifles and shotguns, and silencers. Congress passed it during an era of gangster violence. Owners of these weapons still have to register them with the federal government today.

The Gun Control Act (1968) went much further. It created the system of federally licensed dealers that still exists today. Anyone who sells guns as a business must get a federal license. The law also created "prohibited person" categories. Convicted felons, for example, cannot legally buy a gun.

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act (1993) added a new requirement. Licensed dealers must run a background check before selling a gun. This stops many prohibited buyers before a sale happens. A national instant-check system now runs these checks in minutes.Wikipedia, citing Pub. L. 103-159

The federal assault weapons ban (1994) banned certain semi-automatic weapons and large ammunition magazines. But the law had a built-in expiration date. It was written to last only ten years. Congress did not pass a new law to extend it. So the ban expired on its own in 2004, and it has not been renewed since.Wikipedia, citing Pub. L. 103-322

These four laws are still the foundation of federal gun policy today. The next section looks at how big the problem they were meant to address really is.
