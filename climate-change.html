Frozen Time Capsules: What Ice Can Tell Us About Climate Change

Scientists drill deep into ancient ice to find real air bubbles from 800,000 years ago. Here's what that ice reveals — and why what's happening today looks so different.

Washington's climate policy, right now

Here's a plain, dated snapshot as of July 22, 2026. The Climate Commitment Act remains in place statewide — Washington voters rejected a 2024 ballot measure to repeal it (see the Washington's Climate Story section above for the full background). src

State law requires Washington to cut total greenhouse gas emissions to 45% below 1990 levels by 2030 — roughly 51 million metric tons per year. src The state's most recent official inventory, released by the Department of Ecology, shows total emissions at 96.1 million metric tons in 2022 — a 0.5% decline from 2021's 96.6 million metric tons. src

People who track this closely read that gap differently. Todd Myers of the Washington Policy Center has said the state would need annual emissions cuts of 5-7% to hit the 2030 target — far above the 0.5% most recently recorded — and called the state "nowhere near on track." src Environmental advocate Leah Missik has pointed to "significant steps" already taken, while agreeing more progress is still needed to meet the target. src This page reports both readings rather than picking one — the numbers above are the same regardless of which characterization you find more persuasive.

Recent events, in context

This page's earlier sections explained how scientists study climate over long timescales — hundreds of thousands of years of ice-core data, and nearly 70 years of direct CO2 measurement. A single heat wave or a single drought year, on its own, isn't proof of a long-term trend any more than one warm day in March proves winter is over.

What scientists actually track is whether events like these are becoming more frequent or more intense over time, using the same kind of long-running, direct measurement discussed in Section 3. The events in the timeline above are simply this page's honest, dated snapshot of "what's happening right now" — not a claim that any single event, by itself, proves anything about long-term climate trends.

Question 1Deep-Time Climate History

Picture a drink with a really long, skinny straw — one that's 800,000 years long. That's basically what scientists drink from when they study old ice. Antarctica's ice sheet has been building up, layer by layer, for hundreds of thousands of years. Drill straight down through it and you're pulling up a frozen timeline, with the oldest ice at the bottom.

Nobody had weather instruments 800,000 years ago. Nobody was writing anything down. So how do scientists know what the climate was like that far back? They read clues left behind in nature — clues called climate proxies.

📖 Key Word: Climate Proxy — Natural evidence that records information about past climate, standing in for a direct measurement nobody was around to take. Ice cores, ocean and lake sediment layers, tree rings, and coral growth bands are all climate proxies. src

Ice cores — the long cylinders of ice scientists drill out of a glacier or ice sheet — are one of the best climate proxies we have. Here's why: every year, a fresh layer of snow falls on top of the ice in places like Antarctica and Greenland. Summer snow and winter snow pack down differently, so each year leaves its own stripe, like a tree ring. src Count the stripes down from the top, and you know exactly how old the ice at any depth is.

It gets better. As each year's snow gets buried under the next year's snow, tiny bubbles of air get trapped and sealed inside the ice. src Those bubbles are real, actual samples of ancient air — a direct sample of exactly what gases, including carbon dioxide (CO2), were floating around the year that ice formed. The ice itself holds another clue too: colder years leave a slightly different chemical fingerprint than warmer years, so scientists can also use the ice to estimate how cold or warm it was when that snow fell. src

The longest unbroken ice-core record on Earth comes from a site in Antarctica called EPICA Dome C. It reaches back roughly 800,000 years. src NASA reports that within just that stretch of time, Earth's climate has cycled through eight full ice ages, each followed by a warmer period — over and over again, like clockwork. src A second, shorter record called the Vostok ice core, drilled at a different Antarctic site, backs up the same story going back about 420,000 years. src

So what do these long ice-core records show? Earth's climate has always run in cycles: cold ice age, warm "interglacial" period, cold again, on repeat. None of this was caused by people — humans weren't around yet, or were too few and too low-tech to change a whole planet's atmosphere. So what was driving it? Scientists point to natural causes: slow wobbles and tilts in Earth's orbit (called Milankovitch cycles) that change how much sunlight different parts of the planet get, plus volcanic eruptions and small shifts in the sun's own energy output. src

Atmospheric CO₂ over the past 800,000 years, based on the EPICA Dome C ice core (Antarctica): CO₂ rose and fell through repeated natural ice ages but never topped 300 ppm in all that time src — until today's measured level of 431 ppm. src

Keep this chart in mind — it's the page's first big clue. This section is only about the natural, ice-age part of that story. Section 3 comes back to explain exactly what's different about the spike at the very end.

Question 2The Greenhouse Effect

Ever gotten into a car that's been sitting in the sun with the windows rolled up? It's way hotter inside than the air outside. Sunlight comes in through the glass, warms up the seats and dashboard, and then that heat has a hard time getting back out. Earth's atmosphere does something surprisingly similar — and without it, Earth would be a frozen, lifeless rock.

Here's how it actually works. Sunlight travels through the atmosphere and warms Earth's surface — the ground, the oceans, everything. That warmed surface then tries to release the heat back out toward space. But certain gases in the atmosphere catch some of that outgoing heat and send it back down instead of letting it escape. src Scientists call these greenhouse gases, because they trap heat the same way the glass walls of a greenhouse do.

📖 Key Word: Greenhouse Gas — A gas in the atmosphere that traps heat instead of letting it escape to space. Carbon dioxide (CO2), methane, and water vapor are the main ones. src

This is normally a good thing — a really good thing. Without any greenhouse gases at all, Earth's average surface temperature would plunge by about 33°C (59°F). src Instead of the mild planet we live on, with an average temperature around 15°C (59°F), Earth would be locked in a deep freeze — too cold for the life we know to exist. src The greenhouse effect is exactly what makes Earth livable in the first place.

The greenhouse effect in three steps: sunlight arrives, the surface warms and releases heat, and greenhouse gases trap some of that heat instead of letting it all escape to space. src

So greenhouse gases aren't the villain of this story — not by themselves. Earth needs some of them. The question that actually matters is: what happens when there's a lot more of them than there used to be? That's exactly what the next section digs into.

Question 3What's Different This Time

You just read about huge climate swings that happened over thousands of years. Ice ages came and went, CO2 rose and fell, all driven by slow natural causes like Earth's orbit. So here's the question this section actually answers: if climate has always changed, what's different about the change happening right now?

The short answer: speed. Past natural warming after an ice age happened gradually, over thousands of years. NASA reports that the warming happening today is occurring roughly 10 times faster than that average natural post-ice-age warming rate — and that today's pace hasn't been matched at any point in at least the past 10,000 years. src The CO2 rise is even more lopsided: NASA reports that CO2 from human activity is increasing about 250 times faster than it did from natural sources coming out of the last ice age. src

How do scientists know this so precisely? Because unlike the deep past, this part of the story wasn't reconstructed from ice cores after the fact — it was measured directly, as it happened. In March 1958, a scientist named Charles David Keeling set up an instrument at the Mauna Loa Observatory in Hawaii and started taking continuous, direct measurements of CO2 in the air. src Nobody has stopped since. It's the longest unbroken record of direct atmospheric CO2 measurement on Earth, and scientists call the result the Keeling Curve. src

📖 Key Word: Keeling Curve — The continuous, direct record of atmospheric CO2 measured at Mauna Loa Observatory since 1958, named for the scientist who started it. It's a real-time measurement, not a reconstruction from proxies like ice cores. src

When Keeling's measurements began in 1958, CO2 in the atmosphere stood at 315 parts per million. src By June 2026, that number had climbed to 431 parts per million — a steady, uninterrupted climb the entire time, with no leveling off. src

The Keeling Curve: continuous, directly measured atmospheric CO₂ at Mauna Loa Observatory, Hawaii, from 315 ppm in 1958 to 431 ppm in June 2026 — a steady climb with no plateau. src src

Put the two charts side by side — Section 1's 800,000-year ice-core record and this section's Keeling Curve — and the difference jumps out. Over 800,000 years, natural CO2 cycled up and down but never crossed 300 ppm. In less than 70 years of direct measurement, it's shot up to 431 ppm and is still climbing. That's the pattern scientists point to when they talk about what's different this time.

Question 4Effects Being Observed

These aren't abstract numbers on a chart — they're changes scientists can actually go out and measure, in places you can visit. Here are two, one global and one right in our own backyard.

Sea levels are rising, worldwide. As ocean water warms, it expands, and melting ice sheets and glaciers add even more water to the ocean. Satellites have tracked global sea level rise since 1993, and the rate has been speeding up: it was about 2.1 millimeters per year in 1993, and had climbed to about 4.5 millimeters per year by 2024 — more than double. src Altogether, sea level rose about 11.1 centimeters (roughly 4.4 inches) between 1993 and 2023. src

Now for our backyard: Mount Rainier's glaciers are shrinking. Mount Rainier, less than 60 miles from Seattle, is the most heavily glaciated peak in the continental United States — and scientists have been able to directly measure how much of that ice has disappeared. The U.S. Geological Survey compared detailed surveys of the mountain from 1970 and from 2007/2008 and found that Mount Rainier had lost about 14% of its glacial ice and snow volume in those 37-38 years. src Nearly all of the mountain's glaciers got thinner and retreated up-slope, especially at lower elevations and on slopes that face south, toward more direct sun. src

Rising seas and shrinking glaciers are two different kinds of evidence, measured in two totally different ways — one from orbiting satellites, one from a scientist standing on a mountain with survey equipment. They point to the same conclusion: the warming isn't just numbers in a report. It's visibly reshaping real coastlines and real glaciers, including ones close to home.

Close to HomeWashington's Climate Story

The Climate Commitment Act

In 2021, Washington's governor signed a law called the Climate Commitment Act (CCA). src It's a cap-and-invest program: the state sets a shrinking limit ("cap") on how much greenhouse gas the state's largest polluters are allowed to release, and lowers that limit over time. src Companies that need to emit more than their share have to buy "allowances" at auction — and that auction money becomes the program's revenue. src The program's first auction was held in 2023. src

You may also hear the CCA called a "gas tax" in everyday conversation. That's worth untangling, because Washington actually has two separate things affecting what you pay at the pump: a traditional state fuel tax that funds road construction and maintenance, and the CCA's cap-and-invest program, which is a different kind of program with a different purpose (reducing emissions, not funding roads) even though its costs can also show up in the price of gas. src Exactly how many cents per gallon the CCA adds has been debated — Washington state's own early official estimate and independent analyses since have landed on different numbers — so this page won't state one specific figure as settled fact.

Where does the CCA's auction money go? By law, it has to fund specific kinds of projects: clean transportation, climate resilience, and programs in communities most affected by pollution — with at least 35% required to benefit overburdened communities and at least 10% required to have Tribal support. src The Washington State Department of Ecology reported that during the 2023–2025 budget period, 37 state agencies had invested roughly $1.5 billion of CCA revenue into projects including clean transportation, wildfire resiliency, salmon habitat restoration, and household energy-bill assistance. src

In November 2024, Washington voters decided the CCA's fate directly, through a ballot measure called Initiative 2117, which would have repealed the entire program. Voters rejected the repeal: about 62% voted against Initiative 2117, and about 38% voted for it, so the Climate Commitment Act stayed in place. src Supporters and opponents of the measure together spent well over $13 million campaigning before the vote. src

Washington's Electricity Mix

Flip on a light switch in Washington, and there's a good chance the electricity came from a river. In 2024, hydropower — electricity generated by the force of moving water — made up roughly 60% of Washington's own electricity generation, more than any other single source. src Washington generates more hydropower in total than any other U.S. state — about a quarter of the entire country's utility-scale hydropower in 2024 came from Washington alone. src

A big reason why: the Grand Coulee Dam, on the Columbia River in eastern Washington. It's the largest power plant of any kind in the United States. In 2024, it alone generated about 15.4 million megawatt-hours of electricity — enough to help supply eight western states and parts of Canada. src

📖 Key Word: Hydropower — Electricity generated by using the force of flowing or falling water, usually through dams, to spin turbines. It produces no direct carbon dioxide emissions while generating electricity. src

Washington's Carbon Footprint

A carbon footprint is the total amount of greenhouse gas that an activity, a person, or a whole state's economy is responsible for putting into the atmosphere, usually measured in metric tons. It's a way of comparing how much different places or activities contribute to the buildup of greenhouse gases.

In 2019, Washington's per-person greenhouse gas footprint was about 13.5 metric tons of CO2 equivalent — down 15% from where it stood in 2005. src That same year, the U.S. national average per-person footprint was about 17.6 metric tons, according to EPA data. src In other words, the average Washingtonian's footprint that year was noticeably smaller than the average American's.

This connects directly back to the electricity mix above. Producing electricity by burning fossil fuels (like natural gas or coal) releases CO2; producing it with hydropower generally doesn't. Since Washington gets such an unusually large share of its electricity from hydropower instead of fossil fuels, the state's electricity sector tends to release less CO2 per person than states that rely more heavily on fossil fuels for power. That's a structural fact about the state's energy mix — not a claim about what any individual Washingtonian personally does or doesn't do.
