Ceasefire Collapses as Fighting Resumes

In early July 2026, fighters from Iran's Revolutionary Guard, a branch of Iran's military that answers directly to its Supreme Leader, struck commercial tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway between Iran and Oman that a fifth of the world's oil passes through. The U.S. retaliated with strikes across Iran, and President Trump declared the ceasefire "over." Fighting has continued for more than a week. See the full situation update below.Jump to update

How is the conflict spreading beyond Iran?

For most of April, May, and June, the Gulf states, Lebanon, and the Strait of Hormuz were quiet fronts. The April 8 ceasefire held, and a June 17 peace memorandum seemed to be moving both sides toward a lasting deal.Al Jazeera

That changed on July 6–8. Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck commercial tankers near Oman, and the U.S. answered with strikes on more than 80 targets inside Iran. President Trump declared the ceasefire "over."CNN

The Strait of Hormuz is shut again. Iran declared the waterway closed "until the end of U.S. interference in this region," and tanker traffic has fallen to a small fraction of normal. The U.S. Navy disputes that the strait is fully closed and is blockading Iranian ports instead.Al Jazeera

The Gulf front has reopened, too. U.S. strikes on Iran have now hit six nights in a row. Iran fired missiles and drones at U.S.-linked bases in Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar, and Oman. A child in Qatar was hurt by falling shrapnel. Jordan's air defenses shot down three Iranian missiles passing through its airspace; Iraq and Syria shot down drones and missiles too.Al Jazeera

This is not a quiet standoff. Iran's Foreign Ministry says it has "no plans" to return to talks. Qatar and Pakistan are trying to restart negotiations anyway.Al Jazeera

Think About It

When a conflict spreads to countries that weren't originally involved, it makes it much harder to stop. Why do you think Iran struck U.S. allies in the Gulf again, instead of only striking the U.S. and Israel directly?

The Strait of Hormuz — shut down again. About 20% of the world's oil and gas normally passes through this narrow waterway. On July 12, only 14 ships crossed, down from 37 the same day the week before and more than 100 a day before the war started. Iran says the strait is closed; the U.S. Navy disputes that and is blockading Iranian ports instead.CNBC

The human cost keeps rising: Cross-source counts put total deaths across the war at somewhere between about 7,000 and 9,700+, with tens of thousands more wounded. Iran's own count of its dead is over 3,400; U.S. and Israeli estimates run much higher.Wikipedia In the week after the ceasefire collapsed, U.S. forces struck roughly 140 targets inside Iran. The economic pain still reaches far beyond the Middle East. Higher oil prices push up the cost of gas, food, and shipping for people everywhere — not just in countries that are fighting.Al Jazeera

Key Word — Strait of Hormuz

A narrow sea passage between Iran and Oman. It is one of the most important shipping routes in the world because so much oil and gas passes through it. When it is blocked, energy prices rise everywhere.

How are people reacting — in the U.S. and around the world?

Two big stories about reactions inside Iran stand out this month: a massive funeral for the leader killed in February, and protests that came back the moment it ended.

A huge, days-long funeral: In early July, Iran held an enormous public funeral for Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, the Supreme Leader killed at the start of the war. His body was carried through Tehran, Qom, the Iraqi holy cities of Najaf and Karbala, and finally to his hometown of Mashhad for burial. Iranian officials say millions of people turned out.NPR

The new Supreme Leader still hasn't been seen. Mojtaba Khamenei, the slain leader's son and successor, did not appear at his own father's funeral. He was reportedly hurt in the same strike that killed his father, and officials say keeping him out of sight is meant to protect him from assassination. Some Iranians say his absence makes them feel less secure, not more.Al Jazeera

Protests resumed as soon as the funeral ended. By the weekend of July 11–12, demonstrations broke out in cities across Iran, including Tehran, Ahvaz, Shiraz, Kermanshah, and Shush. Retirees, factory and healthcare workers, and university students all took to the streets — not mainly about the war, but about years of high inflation, unpaid pensions, and falling living standards.Iran News Update

"Our tables are empty": That was one chant retirees used, alongside slogans like "enough of warmongering." Protesters are frustrated that the government keeps talking about the war while everyday costs keep rising, and Iran's economy is expected to shrink sharply this year.IranWire

Around the region: Iran traded strikes with the U.S. and Gulf states through mid-July. Qatar and Bahrain didn't condemn Iran outright. Instead, Qatar raised its security threat level, and Gulf militaries took defensive action. At the same time, Qatar kept working with Pakistan to try to broker a new ceasefire.Al Jazeera

Think About It

Protesters are angry about the economy, not just the war — but the war is a big reason the economy is struggling. Why might a government leaning on wartime unity make it harder, not easier, to keep people from protesting economic problems?

Iran's most powerful government position, with final say over the military, courts, and major decisions — even more powerful than the elected president. Until March 2026, this was Ayatollah Ali Khamenei.

The protest movement returned in July 2026, right after Khamenei's funeral, driven by the same economic hardship.NPR

A heavy early loss

Iran reported that 153 civilians were killed at a girls' elementary school in southern Iran in the opening days of the war. The Israeli military denied being responsible.PBS

The "Woman, Life, Freedom" movement (see the 2022 timeline entry below) grew out of decades of smaller protests. Who Are the People of Iran?

The Haft-sin — a traditional table set for Nowruz, the Persian New Year. Over 300 million people worldwide celebrate Nowruz. Wikimedia Commons (CC-BY)
Important

The Iranian government ≠ the Iranian people. Millions of Iranians love art, music, poetry, soccer, and connection with the world. Many have been protesting against their own government's restrictions. When news talks about "Iran doing something," it usually means the government of Iran, not everyday Iranians.

A gathering outside the Qom Seminary, Iran's biggest center for Shia religious study. Government-organized rallies like this are common there — a reminder that images from Qom often show official events, not everyday classes. Wikimedia Commons (CC-BY 4.0)

Iran has a very young population — about half of all Iranians are under 35 years old.Nat Geo Kids Many young Iranians grew up using social media and the internet to connect with the world, even though the government often tries to restrict what they can see online.UConn

Iran is home to many ethnic groups: Persians, Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Arabs, and Lurs.Nat Geo Kids It has a rich tradition of poetry, art, and philosophy going back thousands of years — Persian poets like Rumi and Hafez are still read all over the world today.

Section 4

How Did We Get Here? The History Timeline

Why does history matter here?

Many tensions between Iran and the United States go back 70+ years. Understanding what happened then helps explain why things are so complicated today.

Around 550 BCE

The Persian Empire

Long before it was called Iran, this region was home to the mighty Persian Empire — one of the largest empires in history, stretching from Egypt to India.Nat Geo Kids Persia was known for its science, law, art, and culture. That long history is a big reason Iranians today are very proud of their ancient heritage.

Early 1900s

Foreign Powers Eye Iran's Oil

Iran was ruled by kings called Shahs. Britain and Russia pressured Iran because they wanted access to its enormous oil reserves and trade routes.NPR Iranians began pushing for more control over their own country and resources — tension that would boil over in 1953.

1951–1953

Iran's Democracy Is Overthrown — With CIA Help

Mohammad Mosaddegh, removed from power in 1953. Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Iran elected a very popular prime minister named Mohammad Mosaddegh.NPR He wanted Iran to control its own oil instead of letting Britain profit from it. Most Iranians thought that seemed very reasonable.

Britain was furious. They worked with the CIAThe Central Intelligence Agency — the U.S. government's spy agency. to secretly remove Mosaddegh from power and put the Shah back in control, in an operation called Operation Ajax.NPR Many Iranians never forgot this. It's one of the biggest reasons for deep distrust of the United States in Iran today.

→ NPR: How the CIA overthrew Iran's democracy in four days
1979

The Islamic Revolution

Ayatollah Khomeini at Mehrabad Airport, Tehran, 1979. Wikimedia Commons (GFDL)

After years of anger at the Shah's corrupt government — including human rights abuses and repression — massive protests swept Iran, and the Shah fled the country.NPR A religious leader named Ayatollah Khomeini took control. Iran became an Islamic Republic: a government run according to strict religious law.NPR

→ NPR Throughline: Iran and the U.S. — Part One
1979–1981

The Hostage Crisis

Iranian students stormed the U.S. Embassy in Tehran and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days.NPR The two countries have had no normal diplomatic relations since. That means they have no embassies in each other's countries and rarely communicate directly.

1980–1988

The Iran-Iraq War

Iraq, led by Saddam Hussein, invaded Iran. The war lasted 8 brutal years and killed hundreds of thousands of people on both sides.NPR The U.S. quietly supported Iraq during this war, which added more resentment toward the United States in Iran.

→ NPR: Iran and the U.S. — Part Two: Rules of Engagement
2000s–2010s

Iran's Nuclear Program Becomes a Global Issue

Bushehr Nuclear Power Plant, Iran's main nuclear facility. Wikimedia Commons (CC-BY-SA)

Iran began developing nuclear technology, which it said was for electricity and energy.CFR Many countries — especially the U.S. and Israel — worried it was secretly building nuclear weapons. The U.S. and other countries put sanctions on Iran, which made life much harder for ordinary Iranians.

Key Word — Sanctions

When countries agree not to trade with or do business with another country, to pressure it to change its behavior. Sanctions hurt ordinary people, not just the government.

2015

The Nuclear Deal

The U.S., Iran, and other world powers signed a major agreement: Iran would limit its nuclear program, and the world would lift economic sanctions.CFR It seemed like a turning point. Ordinary Iranians hoped their lives would get better — but it was controversial, and that hope didn't last long.

→ CFR Education: Background on U.S.-Iran Relations
2018

The U.S. Withdraws From the Nuclear Deal

President Trump withdrew the United States from the 2015 nuclear agreement and put new, much harsher sanctions back on Iran.CFR The Iranian economy suffered badly, and tensions between the U.S. and Iran spiked quickly.

January 2020

The U.S. Kills Iran's Top General

The U.S. military killed General Qasem Soleimani, Iran's most powerful military commander, in a drone strike in Iraq.NPR Iran was outraged, and many feared it would start a major war. Iran launched missiles at U.S. military bases in Iraq in response, injuring over 100 American soldiers.NPR

→ NPR: Who was Soleimani and why did his killing matter?
2022

"Woman, Life, Freedom" Protests

Solidarity protest in Amsterdam, October 2022. Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)

A 22-year-old Kurdish-Iranian woman named Mahsa Amini died after being arrested by Iran's "morality police" for not wearing her hijab the way the government required.NPR Massive protests erupted across Iran — especially among women — demanding freedom and an end to strict dress code laws. The slogan "Woman, Life, Freedom" echoed in streets worldwide.

Dec 2025–Jan 2026

Iran's currency collapsed, prices skyrocketed, and people went hungry. Hidden History Unlocked

1960s — The Shah's White Revolution: Between the 1953 coup and the 1979 revolution, the Shah launched major modernization programs — building roads, schools, and giving women the right to vote. But he also used a brutal secret police force called SAVAK to silence critics. Many historians say this repression actually helped cause the 1979 revolution, because people grew more and more angry.

Section 5

Why Does This Matter — Especially for Us?

Oil refinery in Iran. Iran holds the world's 4th largest oil reserves. Wikimedia Commons, public domain
Iran's oil reserves

Iran has the 4th largest oil reserves on Earth.Wikipedia When there is conflict in Iran, it can affect oil prices — and gas prices — everywhere, including here in the U.S.

A conflict that could spread

Iran has allies and partners across the Middle East — in Lebanon, Gaza, Iraq, and Yemen.Princeton When the U.S. and Israel attack Iran, Iran can respond through these partners — spreading the conflict to more places and countries.

U.S. actions have a long history

The United States has been involved in Iran's story for over 70 years — from helping overthrow Iran's democracy in 1953, to the nuclear deal, to the killing of Soleimani, to the 2026 attacks.NPR

Real people are affected every day

Behind every news headline are millions of ordinary Iranians — students, parents, teachers, and kids. Many of them want the same things people everywhere want: safety, freedom, and a chance at a better life.UConn

Media literacy matters here

Multiple experts warn that reporting on Iran can be incomplete or biased. Iran's government often restricts internet access and controls what information gets out.UConn That's why it's important to check multiple trusted sources and think critically about what you read.

5 Points Unlocked — Bonus Deep Dive

Iran's government is actually a hybrid system — it has an elected president AND parliament, BUT an unelected Supreme Leader has the final say over everything. Iranians can vote, but the Supreme Leader can override or block any decision he disagrees with. After Khamenei's death in 2026, no one knows who will take over — or what it will mean for Iran's future.

10 Points — You're a History Expert

You've shown serious curiosity about this topic. Here's the hardest question to think about: the U.S. helped remove Iran's democracy in 1953. Do you think that decision, made 70+ years ago, still shapes U.S.-Iran relations today? If a country makes a mistake, how long should other countries remember it — and should it affect how they work together now?

There's no single right answer. Historians, politicians, and Iranians themselves disagree. That's what makes it a great question.
