Understanding Iran

A 60-day peace clock ran out. Iran and Oman sketched a new shipping lane. The strait that carries a fifth of the world's oil is still barely moving.

Iran is an old country with a young population. For most of the last fifty years it has been at odds with the United States. In 2026 that turned into open fighting. What follows is what happened, how the two countries got here, and why a narrow strip of water off Iran's coast can change the price of fuel in Washington.

Start with the water, because it explains why the rest of the world pays attention. The Strait of Hormuz is the only sea route out of the Persian Gulf. It is narrow. Iran sits along one whole side of it. And close to a fifth of the oil the world burns goes through it every day. That geography hands Iran a lever it would not otherwise have, and it is why a fight there is never only a local fight.

The quarrel behind it is older than any of this. In 1953 the United States and Britain helped remove Iran's elected prime minister and put the Shah back in power. The Shah was friendlier to Western oil interests. In 1979 Iranians overthrew the Shah. Students then seized the U.S. embassy in Tehran and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days. The two countries have not had normal relations since. So every argument that follows — over nuclear work, over sanctions, over who is threatening whom — is being had by two countries that already do not trust each other.

The argument that has dominated the last twenty years is about nuclear technology. Iran built a nuclear programme and said it was for electricity. The United States, Israel and others suspected it was also a path to a weapon. In 2015 Iran and several world powers signed a deal. Iran would limit the programme, and sanctions on its economy would be lifted. In 2018 the United States pulled out of that deal and put harsher sanctions back on. Did that make Iran less dangerous, or more? People who follow this closely genuinely disagree.

It is worth separating the government from the people while you read. Final say in Iran rests with the Supreme Leader, who has authority over the military and the courts, and many Iranians have protested against their own government at real personal risk — in 2009, in 2019, and again in 2022. A country is not the same thing as the people who run it, and that is as true of Iran as it is anywhere else.

Two more things make this less distant than it looks. Iran holds some of the largest oil reserves on Earth, so a threat to the strait moves fuel prices everywhere, including at the pump here. And the United States has been involved in Iran's story for over seventy years, under presidents of both parties. That is why American forces are already stationed nearby when something starts, rather than being sent after it does.

Where things stand, August 2026. Fighting began in February 2026 with U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran. A ceasefire in April held for a few months, then collapsed in July when tankers were struck in the Strait of Hormuz and the U.S. struck back. A peace memorandum signed in June gave the two sides 60 days to reach a lasting deal; that deadline expired on August 17, 2026 without one. Iran and Oman have since agreed a temporary shipping route, so the strait is open — but barely moving. This is a live situation, so anything after August 27, 2026 belongs to a news source rather than to a page like this one.

How is the conflict spreading beyond Iran?

For most of April, May, and June, the Gulf states, Lebanon, and the Strait of Hormuz were quiet fronts. The April 8 ceasefire held, and a June 17 peace memorandum seemed to be moving both sides toward a lasting deal.Al Jazeera

That changed on July 6–8. Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck commercial tankers near Oman, and the U.S. answered with strikes on more than 80 targets inside Iran. President Trump declared the ceasefire "over."CNN

The Strait of Hormuz is still barely moving. Iran declared the waterway closed after July fighting, and the U.S. Navy has kept a blockade on Iranian ports. President Trump said on August 18 that the strait was "open and operating." Ship-tracking data told a quieter story: just three vessels crossed on Sunday, August 16.CNBC A week later, traffic was still a trickle. Ship-tracking firm Kpler counted five confirmed crossings on Tuesday, August 25 — down from more than 130 vessels a day before the war. All five used the route Iran has designated. Another oil tanker was struck by an unidentified projectile that week.CNBC

Iran and Oman sketched a new lane anyway. After talks in Tehran on August 26, Iran's deputy foreign minister said the two countries had agreed on a temporary transit corridor about seven miles wide, plus a project to clear mines. Iran still said the strait would not fully reopen until the United States met conditions from the expired June memorandum — including lifting the naval blockade and sanctions.Al Jazeera

The Gulf front is not quiet either. On August 18, the United Arab Emirates said its air defenses detected two ballistic missiles launched from Iran toward the UAE. One fell outside UAE waters; the other fell inside them. No injuries were reported.CNBC

As of August 27, there is still no new U.S.–Iran peace deal. Qatar's prime minister was in Tehran that day to talk about de-escalation and navigation through Hormuz. President Trump said in an interview that week he had "no time schedule" for restarting talks with Iran.CNBC Iran's foreign minister has said the June memorandum is no longer relevant because, in Iran's view, the U.S. broke it weeks after it was signed. Pakistan says it is still trying to get both sides back to the table.AP News

Key Word — Strait of Hormuz

A narrow sea passage between Iran and Oman. It is one of the most important shipping routes in the world because so much oil and gas passes through it. When it is blocked, energy prices rise everywhere.

How are people reacting — in the U.S. and around the world?

Two big stories about reactions inside Iran stand out this month: a massive funeral for the leader killed in February, and protests that came back the moment it ended.

A huge, days-long funeral: In early July, Iran held an enormous public funeral for Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, the Supreme Leader killed at the start of the war. His body was carried through Tehran, Qom, the Iraqi holy cities of Najaf and Karbala, and finally to his hometown of Mashhad for burial. Iranian officials say millions of people turned out.NPR

The new Supreme Leader still hasn't been seen. Mojtaba Khamenei, the slain leader's son and successor, did not appear at his own father's funeral. He was reportedly hurt in the same strike that killed his father, and officials say keeping him out of sight is meant to protect him from assassination. Some Iranians say his absence makes them feel less secure, not more.Al Jazeera

Protests resumed as soon as the funeral ended. By the weekend of July 11–12, demonstrations broke out in cities across Iran, including Tehran, Ahvaz, Shiraz, Kermanshah, and Shush. Retirees, factory and healthcare workers, and university students all took to the streets — not mainly about the war, but about years of high inflation, unpaid pensions, and falling living standards.Iran News Update

"Our tables are empty": That was one chant retirees used, alongside slogans like "enough of warmongering." Protesters are frustrated that the government keeps talking about the war while everyday costs keep rising, and Iran's economy is expected to shrink sharply this year.IranWire

Around the region: Iran traded strikes with the U.S. and Gulf states through mid-July. Qatar and Bahrain didn't condemn Iran outright. Instead, Qatar raised its security threat level, and Gulf militaries took defensive action. At the same time, Qatar kept working with Pakistan to try to broker a new ceasefire.Al Jazeera

The Iranian government and the Iranian people are not the same thing. That difference matters when you read the news.

About half of Iranians are under 35. Many have spent years protesting their own government's rules.

How Did We Get Here? The History Timeline

Before you read 6 min

This region was Persia long before it was Iran, and it was one of the largest empires in history.

Outside countries wanted its oil. That pressure, and how Iranians pushed back against it, is where the last seventy years of trouble start.

Why does history matter here?

Many tensions between Iran and the United States go back 70+ years. Understanding what happened then helps explain why things are so complicated today.

Around 550 BCE

The Persian Empire

Long before it was called Iran, this region was home to the mighty Persian Empire — one of the largest empires in history, stretching from Egypt to India.Nat Geo Kids Persia was known for its science, law, art, and culture. That long history is a big reason Iranians today are very proud of their ancient heritage.

Early 1900s

Foreign Powers Eye Iran's Oil

Iran was ruled by kings called Shahs. Britain and Russia pressured Iran because they wanted access to its enormous oil reserves and trade routes.NPR Iranians began pushing for more control over their own country and resources — tension that would boil over in 1953.

1951–1953

Iran's Democracy Is Overthrown — With CIA Help

Mohammad Mosaddegh, removed from power in 1953. Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Iran elected a very popular prime minister named Mohammad Mosaddegh.NPR He wanted Iran to control its own oil instead of letting Britain profit from it. Most Iranians thought that seemed very reasonable.

Britain was furious. They worked with the CIAThe Central Intelligence Agency — the U.S. government's spy agency. to secretly remove Mosaddegh from power and put the Shah back in control, in an operation called Operation Ajax.NPR Many Iranians never forgot this. It's one of the biggest reasons for deep distrust of the United States in Iran today.

1979

The Islamic Revolution

Ayatollah Khomeini at Mehrabad Airport, Tehran, 1979. Wikimedia Commons (GFDL)

After years of anger at the Shah's corrupt government — including human rights abuses and repression — massive protests swept Iran, and the Shah fled the country.NPR A religious leader named AyatollahA senior rank among Shia Muslim scholars of religious law, and a title rather than a name. Iran became an Islamic Republic: a government run according to strict religious law.NPR

1979–1981

The Hostage Crisis

Iranian students stormed the U.S. Embassy in Tehran and held 52 Americans hostage for 444 days.NPR The two countries have had no normal diplomatic relations since. That means they have no embassies in each other's countries and rarely communicate directly.

1980–1988

The Iran-Iraq War

Iraq, led by Saddam Hussein, invaded Iran. The war lasted 8 brutal years and killed hundreds of thousands of people on both sides.NPR The U.S. quietly supported Iraq during this war, which added more resentment toward the United States in Iran.

2000s–2010s

Iran's Nuclear Program Becomes a Global Issue

Bushehr Nuclear Power Plant, Iran's main nuclear facility. Wikimedia Commons (CC-BY-SA)

Iran began developing nuclear technology, which it said was for electricity and energy.CFR Many countries — especially the U.S. and Israel — worried it was secretly building nuclear weapons. The U.S. and other countries put sanctions on Iran, which made life much harder for ordinary Iranians.

Key Word — Sanctions

When countries agree not to trade with or do business with another country, to pressure it to change its behavior. Sanctions hurt ordinary people, not just the government.

2015

The Nuclear Deal

The U.S., Iran, and other world powers signed a major agreement: Iran would limit its nuclear program, and the world would lift economic sanctions.CFR It seemed like a turning point. Ordinary Iranians hoped their lives would get better — but it was controversial, and that hope didn't last long.

President Trump withdrew the United States from the 2015 nuclear agreement and put new, much harsher sanctions back on Iran.CFR The Iranian economy suffered badly, and tensions between the U.S. and Iran spiked quickly.

January 2020

The U.S. Kills Iran's Top General

The U.S. military killed General Qasem Soleimani, Iran's most powerful military commander, in a drone strike in Iraq.NPR Iran was outraged, and many feared it would start a major war. Iran launched missiles at U.S. military bases in Iraq in response, injuring over 100 American soldiers.NPR

Why Does This Matter — Especially for Us?

Before you read 3 min

Iran holds some of the world's largest oil reserves, and a threat to the Strait of Hormuz moves gas prices everywhere.

The United States has been part of Iran's story for over seventy years, under presidents of both parties. That long involvement is why American forces are already nearby.

Iran's oil reserves

Iran has the 4th largest oil reserves on Earth.Wikipedia That is why a fight in the Strait of Hormuz shows up at U.S. gas pumps: when the waterway is threatened, oil prices rise everywhere.

A conflict that could spread

Iran has allies and partners across the Middle East — in Lebanon, Gaza, Iraq, and Yemen.Princeton When the U.S. and Israel attack Iran, those partners can pull more countries into the same fight — which is how a war that started in one place becomes harder to stop.

U.S. actions have a long history

The United States has been inside Iran's story for over 70 years — from helping overthrow Iran's democracy in 1953, to the nuclear deal, to the killing of Soleimani, to the 2026 attacks.NPR Those choices are why American soldiers, ships, and diplomats are already nearby when the fighting starts again.

Real people are affected every day

Behind every news headline are millions of ordinary Iranians — students, parents, teachers, and kids. Many of them want the same things people everywhere want: safety, freedom, and a chance at a better life.UConn The protests you read about above are those people, not their government.

Media literacy matters here

Multiple experts warn that reporting on Iran can be incomplete or biased. Iran's government often restricts internet access and controls what information gets out.UConn When a government can shut off the internet, checking more than one trusted source is how you find out what you are missing.

5 Points Unlocked — Bonus Deep Dive

Iran's government is actually a hybrid system — it has an elected president AND parliament, BUT an unelected Supreme Leader has the final say over everything. Iranians can vote, but the Supreme Leader can override or block any decision he disagrees with. After Khamenei's death in 2026, his son Mojtaba was named the new Supreme Leader — and he still has not been seen in public.

10 Points — You're a History Expert

You've shown serious curiosity about this topic. Here's the hardest question to think about: the U.S. helped remove Iran's democracy in 1953. Do you think that decision, made 70+ years ago, still shapes U.S.-Iran relations today? If a country makes a mistake, how long should other countries remember it — and should it affect how they work together now?

There's no single right answer. Historians, politicians, and Iranians themselves disagree. That's what makes it a great question.
